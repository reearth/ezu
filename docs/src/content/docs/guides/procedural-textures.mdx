---
title: Textures from nothing
description: Noise, a few shaping nodes and some geometry make clouds, marble, wood, rust, cobblestones, brick and a whole island — with no data at all.
---

3D artists rarely paint their textures by hand. A brick wall, a marble floor or
a rusty panel is usually *generated*: start from noise, stretch it, bend it,
threshold it, and stack the results until the surface reads as the material.
Nothing is loaded from disk. The texture is a recipe.

An ezu style is the same kind of recipe, which makes this the quickest way to
get a feel for building one. Every image on this page comes from a style with
**no `sources` block** — no map data, no images — just nodes. Once the moves
feel natural, putting a map underneath them is a small step, and the
[last section](#putting-it-on-a-map) takes it.

Each finished texture is a complete style under
[`docs/fixtures/diagrams/`](https://github.com/reearth/ezu/tree/main/docs/fixtures/diagrams).
Render any of them yourself:

```sh
ezu tile --style docs/fixtures/diagrams/texture-marble.json --tile 13/7276/3225 --out marble.png
```

The tile coordinate only picks *which part* of an endless texture you see. Try
another and the pattern carries on.

## The raw material: noise

Almost everything below starts with one op, [`noise`](/ezu/style/nodes/raster/#noise).
It fills the canvas with a random field, and its `type` sets the field's
character:

    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-noise-perlin.webp" alt="Perlin noise: smooth rounded blobs with no grid" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-noise-simplex.webp" alt="Simplex noise: smooth blobs much like Perlin" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-noise-worley.webp" alt="Worley noise: a bright spot at each scattered site, darkening towards the cell edges" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-noise-cell.webp" alt="Cell noise: flat polygonal cells, one grey per cell" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </div>
  <figcaption><code>white</code>, <code>value</code>, <code>perlin</code>; <code>simplex</code>, <code>worley</code>, <code>cell</code>. Each at <code>scale-px: 48</code>.</figcaption>
</figure>

```json
"out": { "op": "noise", "type": "perlin", "scale-px": 48,
         "low-color": "#111111", "high-color": "#f4f4f4" }
```

- **`white`** is a fresh random value per pixel — grain, sparkle, dither.
- **`value`** picks random values on a grid and blends between them. The grid
  shows, which is sometimes what you want.
- **`perlin`** (and `simplex`) blends random *slopes* instead, so the blobs are
  rounded and the grid disappears. This is the workhorse.
- **`worley`** scatters sites over the plane and measures the distance to the
  nearest one: bright on each site, darkening towards where two cells meet.
- **`cell`** uses the same cells but gives each one a single flat random value
  — a start for anything made of pieces.

`scale-px` is the size of a feature in pixels. Give it two numbers, `[x, y]`,
and the noise stretches: that one knob turns blobs into the grain of wood or
the layers of stone, as you will see.

## Octaves: detail on top of detail

A single layer of noise is too smooth to look like anything natural. Real
surfaces have large shapes, smaller shapes on those, and smaller ones still.
`octaves` adds copies of the noise, each at twice the frequency (`lacunarity`)
and a fraction of the strength (`gain`) of the one before. This sum is called
*fractal Brownian motion*, fBm. Now give it colour with
[`color-ramp`](/ezu/style/nodes/raster/#color-ramp):

<figure class="page-fig page-fig--trio">
  <div class="page-fig__images">
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-octaves-1.webp" alt="One octave of Perlin noise: a few large soft blobs" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-octaves-6.webp" alt="Six octaves: the same blobs with ragged, detailed edges" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-clouds.webp" alt="The six-octave field coloured from sky blue to white: clouds" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </div>
  <figcaption>One octave, six octaves, and the six-octave field through a three-stop ramp.</figcaption>
</figure>

```json
"fbm": { "op": "noise", "kind": "scalar", "type": "perlin",
         "scale-px": 320, "octaves": 6, "gain": 0.55 },
"out": { "op": "color-ramp", "field": "@fbm", "space": "lab",
         "stops": [ { "value": -0.2, "color": "#3d7cc9" },
                    { "value": 0.05, "color": "#8db8e8" },
                    { "value": 0.35, "color": "#ffffff" } ] }
```

`kind: "scalar"` makes `noise` emit the raw numbers — a `ScalarField` running
from −1 to 1 — instead of pixels. A field is not a picture yet; it is something
to *decide with*. `color-ramp` decides colour from it. Where the stops sit is
the whole design: all the blue lives below 0.05 and white takes over by 0.35,
so the sky shows through in the hollows of the field.

That is the first move worth keeping: **make a field, then decide what it
means.**

## Marble: stretch, then warp

Marble is layers of sediment that were folded while soft. A recipe can do the
same in that order.

<figure class="page-fig page-fig--trio">
  <div class="page-fig__images">
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-marble-bands.webp" alt="Noise stretched horizontally into soft parallel bands" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-marble-warped.webp" alt="The bands pushed around by a second noise into folded, flowing shapes" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-marble.webp" alt="Cream marble with thin dark veins where the folded bands cross the middle grey" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </div>
  <figcaption>Stretched noise, warped, and a ramp that only darkens a narrow band.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="diagram">
  <img src="/ezu/diagrams/texture-marble.svg" alt="bands (noise) → warped (warp) → lum (pick-channel) → out (color-ramp) → output" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>Four nodes. <code>ezu graph texture-marble.json</code> draws this.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Lay down sediment.** `"scale-px": [900, 70]` makes noise that changes
   slowly sideways and quickly downwards: horizontal bands.
2. **Fold it.** [`warp`](/ezu/style/nodes/raster/#warp) reads each pixel from
   somewhere nearby instead of from where it is, and *where* comes from its own
   noise. This is *domain warping*: the shapes do not change colour, they get
   moved. `amp-px` is how far.
3. **Draw the veins.** [`pick-channel`](/ezu/style/nodes/util/#pick-channel)
   turns the grey picture back into a field, and the ramp does the rest:

```json
"stops": [ { "value": 0.0,  "color": "#d9d4c7" },
           { "value": 0.44, "color": "#f3f0e8" },
           { "value": 0.49, "color": "#8a8478" },
           { "value": 0.5,  "color": "#4a463f" },
           { "value": 0.51, "color": "#8a8478" },
           { "value": 0.56, "color": "#f3f0e8" },
           { "value": 1.0,  "color": "#e4dfd2" } ]
```

Almost the whole range is cream. Only the values right around 0.5 go dark, so
a vein appears wherever the field *crosses* the middle — a thin line that
follows every fold. A ramp is not just a colour scale; a narrow spike in one
is how you draw contour-like lines out of a smooth field.

## Wood: the same moves, different numbers

Wood needs nothing new, only different proportions: noise stretched much
further (`[1400, 26]`), warped only a little, and a ramp from dark late wood
to pale early wood. A second, extremely thin noise (`[220, 1.5]`) laid on with
`multiply` adds the fibres.

<figure class="page-fig">
  <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-wood.webp" alt="Brown wood grain: long wavy rings with fine fibres" width="768" height="768" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>Stretched, gently warped noise through a brown ramp, with a fibre layer multiplied on top.</figcaption>
</figure>

```json
"rings":  { "op": "noise", "type": "perlin", "scale-px": [1400, 26], "octaves": 3, "gain": 0.4,
            "low-color": "#000000", "high-color": "#ffffff" },
"wobble": { "op": "warp", "input": "@rings", "amp-px": 14, "scale-px": 260, "octaves": 2 },
"fibre":  { "op": "noise", "type": "value", "scale-px": [220, 1.5], "octaves": 2,
            "low-color": "#4a2a12", "high-color": "#ffffff" },
"out":    { "op": "blend", "base": "@base", "over": "@fibre", "mode": "multiply", "opacity": 0.35 }
```

Marble and wood share a graph shape. What makes them different materials is the
ratio of the stretch, the strength of the warp and the colours of the ramp.
Most procedural work is this: a handful of shapes, tuned.

## Rust: noise as a mask

So far noise has always become colour. It can just as well decide *where*.
[`threshold`](/ezu/style/nodes/raster/#threshold) cuts a field into 0 and 1, and
`blend` takes a `mask` that says how much of the top layer gets through.

<figure class="page-fig page-fig--pair">
  <div class="page-fig__images">
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-rust-mask.webp" alt="A black and white mask with ragged white patches" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
    <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-rust.webp" alt="Brushed grey metal with orange rust in the same ragged patches" width="384" height="384" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  </div>
  <figcaption>The thresholded field, and the rust layer let through it onto brushed metal.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="diagram">
  <img src="/ezu/diagrams/texture-rust.svg" alt="patches (noise) → mask_f (threshold) → mask (color-ramp) → out (blend); metal (noise) and rust (noise) are the blend's base and over" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>Three noises, three jobs: the metal, the rust, and where the rust goes.</figcaption>
</figure>

```json
"patches": { "op": "noise", "kind": "scalar", "type": "perlin", "scale-px": 160, "octaves": 6, "gain": 0.6 },
"mask_f":  { "op": "threshold", "field": "@patches", "value": 0.1, "softness": 0.08, "low": 0, "high": 1 },
"out":     { "op": "blend", "base": "@metal", "over": "@rust", "mask": "@mask" }
```

The brushed metal is one more stretched noise, this time `[400, 2]`: long thin
streaks. Raise the threshold `value` and the rust retreats; lower it and the
panel corrodes. One number now controls the *amount of wear*, which is the kind
of knob you would expose as a [`$param`](/ezu/style/params/).

## Cobblestones: geometry is procedural too

Noise is not the only generator. Points, cells and lines can be made from
nothing as well, and they are better at anything with crisp, countable pieces.

  </div>
  <figcaption>Seeds, cells and stones; the stones read as a height map; the finished paving.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="diagram diagram--narrow">
  <img src="/ezu/diagrams/texture-cobblestone.svg" alt="The cobblestone graph: point-scatter and tile-bounds, grown by buffer, feed voronoi-fracture, then buffer and smooth make the stones; the stones are filled, blurred and turned into a height field for hillshade; noise layers tone the stone, and the result is stacked over a dark joint colour" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>The trunk builds the shapes; the left branch turns them into light, the right one gives them a surface.</figcaption>
</figure>

**The shapes.**
[`point-scatter`](/ezu/style/nodes/source/#point-scatter) throws random seed
points, [`voronoi-fracture`](/ezu/style/nodes/geometry/#voronoi-fracture) cuts
an area into one cell per seed, a negative
[`buffer`](/ezu/style/nodes/geometry/#buffer) pulls every cell in by 3 px to
open the joints, and [`smooth`](/ezu/style/nodes/geometry/#smooth) rounds the
corners off.

```json
"tile":   { "op": "tile-bounds" },
"area":   { "op": "buffer", "features": "@tile", "distance": 48 },
"seeds":  { "op": "point-scatter", "spacing": 320 },
"cells":  { "op": "voronoi-fracture", "features": "@area", "seeds": "@seeds" },
"shrunk": { "op": "buffer", "features": "@cells", "distance": -3, "join": "round" },
"stones": { "op": "smooth", "features": "@shrunk", "iterations": 3 }
```

The area is the tile grown by 48 px, not the tile itself.
[`tile-bounds`](/ezu/style/nodes/source/#tile-bounds) is exactly the visible
square, and stones cut against it would stop dead at the tile's edge. The
seeds already cover the margin around the tile, so once the area does too,
a stone on the border is cut from the same seeds by both tiles that show it.

`point-scatter` measures its `spacing` in feature-extent units, 4096 to a tile,
so 320 is about 40 px on a 512 px tile. Every other length here is in pixels.

**The light.** This is the trick worth stealing. Fill the stones white and
[`blur`](/ezu/style/nodes/raster/#blur) them: the edges become soft ramps from
0 to 1. Read that as *height* — 0 in the joints, rising to a plateau in the
middle of each stone — and [`hillshade`](/ezu/style/nodes/raster/#hillshade),
which was written for real terrain, lights it:

```json
"silhouette": { "op": "fill-solid", "features": "@stones", "fill": "#ffffff" },
"rounded":    { "op": "blur", "input": "@silhouette", "sigma": 4 },
"coverage":   { "op": "pick-channel", "input": "@rounded", "channel": "a" },
"height":     { "op": "map-range", "field": "@coverage", "out-min": 0, "out-max": 6 },
"relief":     { "op": "hillshade", "field": "@height", "mode": "relief" }
```

`sigma` decides how rounded the stones are, and `out-max` how tall. Any mask can
be turned into relief this way: lettering, field boundaries, a coastline.

**The surface.** Two noises — a broad one for tone, a fine one for grain — are
blended with `clip: true`, which keeps them inside the stones, and the relief is
multiplied over them.

## Brick: regularity is a recipe too

Brick looks like the opposite of noise: a strict pattern. It is still built
from nothing; the pattern is just made of lines instead of fields.

  </div>
  <figcaption>The joints as geometry, stroked flat, then weathered.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="diagram">
  <img src="/ezu/diagrams/texture-brick.svg" alt="The brick graph: tile-bounds, grown by buffer, feeds three hatch nodes; two are cut by dash into alternating head joints; all three are stroked and stacked into mortar, which is warped; separately clay and grain noise, a blurred shadow line and soot noise are blended into the final image" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>The right side lays the bricks; the left side makes them look old.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Laying the bond.** [`hatch`](/ezu/style/nodes/geometry/#hatch) fills a
polygon with parallel lines. Horizontal lines every 32 px are the bed joints.
The vertical joints are the clever part: vertical lines every 72 px, cut by
[`dash`](/ezu/style/nodes/geometry/#dash) into 32 px on, 32 px off, so they only
exist in every other course. A second set is shifted half a brick sideways
(`phase: 0.5`) and one course down (`phase-px: 32`), filling the courses the
first set skipped.

```json
"area":        { "op": "buffer", "features": "@tile", "distance": 64 },
"bed":         { "op": "hatch", "features": "@area", "angle-deg": 0, "spacing": 32 },
"verticals_a": { "op": "hatch", "features": "@area", "angle-deg": 90, "spacing": 72 },
"head_a":      { "op": "dash", "features": "@verticals_a", "dash-px": 32, "gap-px": 32 },
"verticals_b": { "op": "hatch", "features": "@area", "angle-deg": 90, "spacing": 72, "phase": 0.5 },
"head_b":      { "op": "dash", "features": "@verticals_b", "dash-px": 32, "gap-px": 32, "phase-px": 32 }
```

The area is grown past the tile here too, so the joints run into the margin
and the warp below has mortar to pull in from beyond the edge. It grows by
64 px, two courses, for a reason: `dash` counts its pattern from where each
line starts, and a line that starts a whole number of courses above the tile
keeps its dashes on the courses.

Stroke all three in the mortar colour, put them over a flat red, and you have
the middle image — correct, and obviously fake.

**Making it believable.** Nothing in the real world is that clean, and the
weathering is all noise again:

- the clay is a broad noise between two reds instead of one flat colour;
- a very fine noise (`scale-px: 3`) blended with `overlay` gives the fired grit;
- the bed joints are stroked a second time in black, wide, and blurred, and
  that soft line is multiplied in as the shadow under each course;
- `warp` with a tiny `amp-px: 1.5` makes the mortar wander, because nobody lays
  bricks with a ruler;
- one last broad, transparent-to-dark noise multiplies in soot.

Each of those is one or two nodes, and each removes one reason the eye had to
call it computer-made.

## A map of nowhere

Here is where it comes back to maps. fBm is not only a good cloud — it is a
good *terrain*. Map the field to metres and every cartographic op that expects
a DEM accepts it.

<figure class="page-fig">
  <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-terrain.webp" alt="An imaginary landscape: a blue bay with shallows, green lowlands, brown hills and snowy peaks, with hill shading" width="768" height="768" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>Four adjacent tiles of one fBm field, read as elevation. The bay runs across all four borders without a seam.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="diagram">
  <img src="/ezu/diagrams/texture-terrain.svg" alt="fbm (noise) → dem (map-range) → tint (color-ramp), and dem → land (map-range) → relief (hillshade); tint and relief blend into out" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>The same graph a real DEM would use, with <code>noise</code> where the <code>dem</code> source would be.</figcaption>
</figure>

```json
"fbm":    { "op": "noise", "kind": "scalar", "type": "perlin", "scale-px": 420, "octaves": 7, "gain": 0.5 },
"dem":    { "op": "map-range", "field": "@fbm", "in-min": -1, "in-max": 1, "out-min": -1800, "out-max": 2600 },
"land":   { "op": "map-range", "field": "@dem", "in-min": 0, "in-max": 2600, "out-min": 0, "out-max": 2600, "clamp": true },
"relief": { "op": "hillshade", "field": "@land", "mode": "relief", "exaggeration": 0.02, "multidirectional": true }
```

The tint is an ordinary hypsometric ramp — deep blue, shallows, beach, green,
brown, snow — exactly as in
[hillshade and hypsometric tints](/ezu/cookbook/hillshade-and-hypsometric/).
Two details:

- **The sea is flat.** `land` clamps everything below 0 m to 0 before shading,
  so only the land gets relief.
- **The exaggeration is set by eye.** A real DEM knows how many metres a pixel
  covers; a noise field does not, so `hillshade` cannot know how steep "2600 m
  over 420 px" should look. Turn `exaggeration` until it does.

The figure is four tiles side by side, rendered separately. They join because
`noise` defaults to `anchor: world`: the field is defined over the whole world
and each tile looks at its own window onto it, so neighbours agree at the edge.
`warp`, `point-scatter` and `hatch` are anchored the same way, and that is
what lets a texture cover a map rather than a single square — provided, as the
cobblestones and brick showed, the shapes reach past the tile's edge too. More
in [tiles and determinism](/ezu/concepts/tiles-and-determinism/).

## Putting it on a map

Everything above made a texture for the whole tile. A style makes one for the
places that need it, and that is a single `mask`: here, the brick texture from
before, unchanged, let through a fill of real building footprints at z17.

<figure class="page-fig">
  <img src="/ezu/figures/texture-on-map.webp" alt="A city block map where every building footprint is filled with red brick, over cream streets" width="768" height="768" loading="lazy" decoding="async" />
  <figcaption>Brick through a mask of buildings. Tokyo, z17. Map data © OpenStreetMap contributors, © Protomaps.</figcaption>
</figure>

```json
"buildings":  { "op": "features", "source": "basemap", "layer": "buildings" },
"footprints": { "op": "fill-solid", "features": "@buildings", "fill": "#ffffff" },
"out":        { "op": "blend", "base": "@base", "over": "@brick", "mask": "@footprints" }
```

Look across neighbouring buildings and the courses line up: the brick is laid
once, over the whole world, and the buildings are windows onto it. From here
the moves combine freely — a thresholded noise as a mask on top of the
footprints for patchy wear, the blur-and-hillshade trick on the footprints for
raised blocks, marble in a plaza, `point-scatter` instead of `tile-bounds` for
trees.

## Where to go next

- The [node catalog](/ezu/style/nodes/raster/) has a picture for most ops; it is
  the fastest way to find the next ingredient.
- `ezu graph style.json --tile 13/7276/3225 --out graph.html` draws any style's
  graph with every node's own output on it — the best way to see what each step
  of a recipe contributes.
- The [cookbook](/ezu/cookbook/watercolor-fills/) applies the same thinking to
  painterly fills, brush strokes and halftones.
- The [live editor](/ezu/guides/live-editor/) re-renders as you type. Tuning a
  texture is change a number, look, change it again, and it makes that loop
  short.
